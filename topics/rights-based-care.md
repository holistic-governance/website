# Rights-Based Care in Australian Aged Care

> Rights-based care puts the rights of the older person at the centre of how aged care is designed, delivered and regulated. The Aged Care Act 2024, which commenced on 1 November 2025, sets out a Statement of Rights (s 23) and a Statement of Principles (s 25). Neither creates rights or duties enforceable in a court or tribunal (ss 24(3), 26(2)). The Statement of Rights works mainly through a condition of registration (s 144) and complaints to the Complaints Commissioner (s 358).

## What is rights-based care?

Rights-based care means care and services start from the rights of the older person, not the convenience of the registered provider. The Act builds this into its definition of high quality care, which puts the individual first and "upholds the rights of the individual under the Statement of Rights" (s 20).

The Act and the Aged Care Rules 2025 commenced on 1 November 2025. The Act replaced the Aged Care Act 1997, the Aged Care (Transitional Provisions) Act 1997 and the Aged Care Quality and Safety Commission Act 2018.

Rights also run through the Strengthened Aged Care Quality Standards, which applied from 1 November 2025. Standard 1 (The individual) expects person-centred care, dignity, respect, privacy, and support for older people's choices and independence.

## What is the Statement of Rights?

Section 23 sets out the rights in six groups. The full text is in the Act itself, not in the Rules.

- **Independence, autonomy, empowerment and freedom of choice** — to make decisions about their own life, including which services they receive and how, when and by whom; to be supported to make those decisions if needed and have them respected; and to take personal risks (s 23(1)).
- **Equitable access** — to assessment and reassessment that is culturally safe, trauma-aware and healing-informed, and suitable for people living with dementia or other cognitive impairment; and to palliative care and end-of-life care when required (s 23(2)).
- **Quality and safe funded aged care services** — dignity and respect; safe, fair and non-discriminatory treatment; identity, culture, spirituality and diversity valued; services delivered by aged care workers with appropriate qualifications, skills and experience; and freedom from all forms of violence, neglect, coercion, abuse or sexual misconduct (s 23(3)–(4)).
- **Respect for privacy and information** — privacy respected, personal information protected, and access to records and information about their rights, services and costs (s 23(5)–(6)).
- **Person-centred communication and ability to raise issues without reprisal** — to be informed in a way they understand and be heard; to communicate in their preferred language or method, with interpreters and communication aids as required; open communication when issues arise; and to complain without fear of reprisal and have complaints dealt with fairly and promptly (s 23(7)–(9)).
- **Advocates, significant persons and social connections** — support from an advocate or other person of their choice; recognition of carers, visitors and volunteers; staying connected with family, friends, pets and community and, for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, with community, Country and Island Home (s 23(10)–(13)).

## What is the Statement of Principles?

Section 25 sets out the principles of the aged care system under four headings: a person-centred aged care system; an aged care system that values workers and carers; a transparent and sustainable system that represents value for money; and a system that continues to improve. Its first principle is that the safety, health, wellbeing and quality of life of individuals is "the primary consideration" in the delivery of funded aged care services (s 25(1)).

The Principles are directed at government decision-makers, not providers. The Minister, the System Governor, the Commissioner, the Complaints Commissioner and anyone else exercising powers under the Act must have regard to them (s 26(1)). From 1 July 2027 the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Aged Care Commissioner is added to that list. A failure to comply does not affect the validity of a decision (s 26(3)).

## What is the statutory basis?

- **Aged Care Act 2024 (Cth)** — s 20 (high quality care), ss 23–26 (rights and principles), ss 144–145 and 165 (registration conditions), ss 179–180 (statutory duties) and ss 356–358 (Complaints Commissioner).
- **Aged Care Rules 2025**, as amended — operational detail, including which kinds of registered provider the s 144 conditions apply to.
- **Strengthened Aged Care Quality Standards** — especially Standard 1 (The individual), which applies to providers registered in categories 4, 5 and 6.

## Who does it apply to?

- **Older people** — an individual is entitled to the rights in s 23 when accessing, or seeking to access, funded aged care services (s 24(1)).
- **Registered providers** — Parliament intends that registered providers "take all reasonable and proportionate steps to act compatibly" with the rights (s 24(2)). The registration conditions in s 144 apply to registered providers of a kind prescribed by the Rules.
- **Aged care workers and responsible persons** — they are not bound by the Statement of Rights directly. Registered providers must take reasonable steps to make sure their workers and responsible persons comply with the Aged Care Code of Conduct (s 145).
- **Supporters** — a registered supporter may help an older person to do things under the Act, in line with the person's will and preferences (s 27), and must support them only to the extent necessary (s 30(2)).

## What are the key provider obligations?

- **Act compatibly with the rights** — limits may be needed to balance competing rights, the rights of others (including workers) and other laws such as the Work Health and Safety Act 2011 (s 24(2)).
- **Show understanding and have practices in place** — where s 144 applies, demonstrate understanding of the Statement of Rights, have practices to make sure the provider acts compatibly with it, and demonstrate understanding that safety, health, wellbeing and quality of life come first (s 144(1)–(2)).
- **Support decision-making and dignity of risk** — the Act recognises the right to be supported to make decisions and to take personal risks (s 23(1)).
- **Restrictive practices only as a last resort** — to prevent harm, in the least restrictive form for the shortest time, with informed consent, monitoring and review (s 18).
- **Complaints without reprisal** — keep a complaints and feedback management system, do not victimise or discriminate against anyone for complaining, and keep a whistleblower system and policy (s 165).
- **Statutory duty** — ensure, so far as reasonably practicable, that the provider's conduct does not cause adverse effects to people's health and safety (s 179(1)). Weighing what is reasonably practicable includes the rights under the Statement of Rights (s 179(2)(e)).
- **Due diligence by responsible persons** — responsible persons under s 12(1)(a)–(b) must exercise due diligence to make sure the provider complies with its s 179 duty (s 180). Since 31 October 2026, elected members of a local government authority that is a registered provider are not responsible persons under those paragraphs (s 12(3)).

## How are rights enforced?

- **Registration conditions** — the s 144 requirements are conditions of registration, alongside the other conditions in Chapter 3 of the Act.
- **Complaints** — a person may complain to the Complaints Commissioner about a registered provider acting in a way that is incompatible with the Statement of Rights (s 358(a)(ii)). For these complaints the Complaints Commissioner must deal with and resolve the complaint and give the complainant a written complaint determination (s 361(1A)).
- **Whistleblower protections** — disclosures that qualify for protection under s 547 must be managed under the provider's whistleblower system (s 165(1)(e)).
- **Civil penalties for serious failures** — serious failures of the s 179 and s 180 duties attract civil penalties. For a provider that is not an individual, the maximum is 1,000 penalty units, or 4,800 penalty units where the failure results in death, serious injury or illness (s 179(3), (5)).

## What are common provider gaps?

- **Rights described as court-enforceable, or as optional** — neither is right. They work through registration conditions, complaints and the statutory duty.
- **Rights training is a tick-box** — workers are not helped to handle tensions, such as a person's choice to take a risk against clinical advice.
- **Supporters treated as decision-makers** — supporters help the older person decide. They do not decide for them, unless they hold a guardianship, appointment or enduring power covered by s 28.
- **Restrictive practices not systematic** — alternatives not documented, consent recorded but not genuinely informed, and no regular review.
- **Diversity reduced to events** — culture, language and identity not built into assessment, care planning, communication and the workforce.
- **Complaints managed defensively** — treated as a risk rather than information for improvement.
- **Rights not visible to the board** — no reporting on complaints by theme, restrictive practice use or feedback from older people.

## How Holistic Governance supports providers

- **Rights framework review** — gap analysis of policies, practices, training and culture against the Statement of Rights, s 144 and Standard 1.
- **Supported decision-making framework** — how workers and supporters help older people make and communicate their own decisions.
- **Restrictive practices governance** — alternatives, consent, monitoring, review and reduction.
- **Workforce training design** — case-based training on applying the rights and the Aged Care Code of Conduct in daily practice.
- **Partnering with older people** — involving older people and supporters in service design and review.
- **Diversity and cultural safety review** — including care for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people and other diverse groups.
- **Complaints and whistleblower systems** — redesign with board-visible reporting.
- **Power BI dashboards** — rights-aligned measures at board and operational levels.

## Glossary

- **Statement of Rights** — the rights in s 23 of the Aged Care Act 2024 of individuals accessing, or seeking to access, funded aged care services. Not enforceable by proceedings in a court or tribunal (s 24(3)).
- **Statement of Principles** — the principles in s 25 that decision-makers under the Act must have regard to (s 26(1)). Not enforceable by proceedings in a court or tribunal (s 26(2)).
- **High quality care** — care that puts the individual first, upholds their rights under the Statement of Rights and prioritises the matters listed in s 20.
- **Dignity of risk** — the older person's right to take personal risks, including in pursuit of their quality of life, social participation and relationships (s 23(1)(c)).
- **Supporter** — a person registered as a supporter of an individual under s 37, who helps the individual do things under the Act without making decisions for them.
- **Responsible person** — a person responsible for the executive decisions of a registered provider, or with authority or significant influence over its activities, and certain nursing and operational managers (s 12).
- **Restrictive practice** — any practice or intervention that has the effect of restricting the rights or freedom of movement of an individual (s 17).
